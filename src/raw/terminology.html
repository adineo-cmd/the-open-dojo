Terminology

A first class sounds like a language lesson because it is one. These pages collect the words you'll hear most — counting, commands, ranks, and the handful of concepts that don't translate cleanly. Learn them early; everything after gets easier.

Counting, one to ten

“Attention” — heels together, body settled, eyes front.

“Bow” — to the front, to the teacher, to each other.

“Bow to the teacher.”

Kaeru / Owarime

Drill-floor words

Mō ikkai もう一回
“One more time” — the phrase between every repetition.
Yukkuri ゆっくり
“Slowly” — when the instructor wants to see the mechanics, not the speed.
Hayaku 速く
“Faster” — its turn comes later.
Tsugi 次
“Next” — next technique, next pair, next group.
Nigate 苦手
“Weak point” — what your instructor quietly keeps drilling you on.

Greetings that matter

Onegai shimasu お願いします
“Please — I ask this of you.” Said with a bow before training with someone; a request wrapped in trust.
Arigatō gozaimashita ありがとうございました
“Thank you (for what has been done)” — the closing bow to every partner and the class.
Osu 押忍
The multi-purpose syllable of many dojo — greeting, agreement, perseverance. Its etymology is debated; its use is everywhere in some schools and absent in others.

Ranks & titles

Student grades count down — from tenth kyu to first — and then black-belt dan grades count up. The belt colors between white and black differ by school; the words below don't.

TermScriptMeaning
Kyū (grade)級Student rank below black belt; counts down from 10th kyu toward 1st.
Dan (grade)段Black-belt rank; counts up — 1st dan is the beginning, not the summit.
Yudansha有段者“One who holds rank” — a black-belt practitioner.
Sensei (teacher)先生Teacher or instructor — literally “one born before.”
Senpai (senior)先輩Senior student; the person who was once exactly where you are.
Kōhai (junior)後輩Junior student — and someday, everyone below you.
Shihan師範Senior master instructor, typically 5th dan and above.
Renshi / Kyōshi / Hanshi錬士・教士・範士Traditional teaching-license titles awarded in order above dan rank.
Soke宗家Head of a style or school lineage.
Dojō (training hall)道場“Place of the way” — wherever you train, including the garage.
Shinsa (grading)審査A formal belt examination — kihon, kata, and kumite under watchful eyes.
Menkyo kaiden (full license)免許皆伝“License of complete transmission” — the classical systems' highest certificate, given to very few.
Keikogi (uniform)稽古着Training uniform; “gi” for short. Taekwondo's version is the dobok.
Obi (belt)帯The belt — knotted so the ends hang evenly. Yes, that matters.

“Skill earned through time and effort” — martial arts is one application among many.

Sumo vocabulary

A sport with its own complete language — a taste of it:

Rikishi 力士
A sumo wrestler — "strength warrior."
Dohyō 土俵
The ring — a clay platform of consecrated earth and thrown salt.
Mawashi 廻し
The belt — gripping it is the beginning of every technique.
Kimarite 決まり手
The named winning technique — 82 are officially recognized.
Tachi-ai 立ち合い
The initial charge — often the entire fight.
Yokozuna 横綱
Grand champion — the rank above, worn with a white rope belt.

Concepts worth knowing

Some words carry whole philosophies. These five turn up in every Japanese art, and they describe things English takes a sentence to say.

Zanshin 残心

“Remaining mind.” The strike landed — stay aware anyway. Zanshin is the alertness that lasts after the technique, the round, and the test.

Kime 決め

“Decision / focus.” The instant of total commitment at the end of a technique — breath, tension, and intent arriving together.

Ma-ai 間合い

“Interval.” Fighting distance — close enough to act, far enough to react. Every art negotiates it differently; all of them live by it.

Mushin 無心

“No mind.” Action without hesitation or overthinking — the state you drift into after thousands of repetitions, when the body answers first.

Kiai / Kihap 気合・기합

The shout with the strike. Not noise for its own sake: it synchronizes breath and movement, stiffens the core, and unsettles the other side of the exchange.
