Belts & ranking

The belt is younger than most arts that use one. Where it came from, how kyu and dan grades actually count, what each color means in each art — and how to behave on the day you're tested for the next one.

Where belts came from

Before the 1880s there were certificates, not belts: the old Japanese schools awarded scrolls (and eventually licenses such as menkyo kaiden — “complete transmission”) to those who finished a lineage's curriculum. Jigoro Kano, the founder of judo, invented the modern ladder when he borrowed the white/black distinction from the game of go and made it mark beginner and expert. Colored belts between white and black arrived later, popularized in the West — Mikinosuke Kawaishi, teaching judo in Paris, is often credited — because beginners there wanted visible proof of progress. The rest of the martial world followed, which is why a belt system is a borrowing even in arts older than judo.

The unwashed-belt myth: you may have heard that you should never wash your belt because it stores your training. It's folklore — and modern hygiene wins. Wash your belt as often as your gi. Your progress is stored in you, not in the cotton.

How kyu & dan count

The system runs in two directions, which trips up every newcomer: student grades count down, black-belt grades count up.

Kyū 級 — student grades

You enter at a high number and descend: 10th kyu → 1st kyu. Colored belts usually attach to these steps, and the intervals between gradings start long (6+ months) and shorten as fundamentals settle.

Dan 段 — black-belt grades

Shodan (1st dan) is the beginning of serious study, not a finish line. Dans climb 1 → 10; early dan need years each, and the top grades are typically awarded for a lifetime's contribution.

In WT taekwondo, juniors under 15 earn a poom — a junior black-belt rank that converts to dan at adulthood. Kendo goes further and wears no visible rank at all: the kyu/dan system exists, but you can't see it on the floor.

The BJJ road

Brazilian jiu-jitsu runs the slowest, most famous ladder in the martial world — and the most honest about what belts measure: time on the mat and the ability to apply it against resistance.

BeltTypical timeWhat it usually means
White0–2 yearsSurvival mode: learning positions, defending, breathing.
Blue~1–3 yearsThe first real filter — you can control untrained people and struggle productively with everyone else.
Purple~1.5–3 yearsA genuinely dangerous grappler; your own game begins to exist.
Brown~1–2 yearsRefinement — sharpening the game, plugging the gaps.
Black~8–12 years totalMastery of fundamentals and the right to teach them.
Coral & reddecades7th/8th degrees (black-red, red-white) and 9th/10th (red) honor a lifetime.

Stripes (up to four per belt, in most schools) mark progress within a grade. Kids' ranks run on a longer, lighter ladder of their own.

What black belt actually means

Two honest translations: “solid fundamentals” and “ready to begin.” A shodan has kept the promises of the syllabus — attendance, basics, kata, sparring — and now owns the tools instead of borrowing them. What they don't yet have is depth, and every senior rank exists to measure the depth.

Watch out for the shortcut version. Some schools sell fast black belts; the price is that the belt describes the fee, not the fighter. A fair test takes years of attendance and can still be failed. If nobody ever fails a grading, the grading is a receipt, not an exam. See also History & philosophy on what rank traditions were meant to protect.

Grading day

A shinsa (審査) usually runs: warm-up, kihon (basics), kata or forms, then sparring or set applications — with the instructor watching for the whole arc, not the best round.

Etiquette for the day

  • Know your material cold. The test is where nerves live; fundamentals should live in your hands already.
  • Don't ask when you're grading. In most traditions, the instructor promotes you when you're ready — asking is like asking a magician for the trick.
  • Failures are normal. A serious school has them. Treat a retake as part of the syllabus; it is.
  • Cheer the others. The person testing after you is watching how you behave — just like you watched before.
  • Thank the ukes. Whoever took your strikes and fed your attacks earned the bow as much as you did.

Dojo etiquette, the short version

Etiquette isn't decoration — it's the safety and respect system that makes hard training possible between strangers. Customs vary by school; these are near-universal.

Arriving

Shoes off at the mat. Bow at the edge. If you're late, wait at the edge for the instructor's nod rather than walking into the line of fire.

Training

Fingernails and toenails trimmed; jewelry off; feet clean (wash yours after street shoes). Bow to a partner before and after working together — it starts the agreement and ends it.

Leaving

Bow off the mat. If class ends with kneeling and mokuso, stay through it. Help fold the mats if that's the custom — the dojo belongs to everyone who trains in it.
