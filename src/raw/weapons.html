Weapons arts

Unarmed martial arts are the minority tradition. For most of human history, the serious fighting was done with something in the hand — spear, sword, staff — and the “empty-hand” arts grew up around them. Train a weapon and you learn distance, timing, and intent at a scale fists never teach. Everything here is done with safe stand-ins, under instruction, at agreed distances.

Japan & Okinawa

Japan codified its weapon arts into dō (ways) and ryū (schools) with unmatched care; Okinawa preserved its own parallel set, remembered in the weapon kata of karate.

Bokken / bokutō 木剣
The wooden sword — kenjutsu's standard tool, sharp in shape if not in steel.
Shinai 竹刀
Four bamboo slats bound together — kendo's answer to being able to hit fully without killing anyone.
Katana 刀
The curved long sword; iaido studies its draw, cut, and resheath as a moving meditation.
Naginata 薙刀
Halberd with a curved blade — historically the great weapon of samurai women's training, now a modern sport with padded tips.
Yari 槍
The spear — the true queen of the battlefield, whatever the movies say.
Bo / rokushakubō 六尺棒
Six-foot staff — reach, rotation, and thrusts; Okinawan kobudo's centerpiece.
Sai 釵
Metal truncheon with prongs — a trapper and catcher, not a blade.
Tonfa 拐
Side-handled baton — spin it and guard with it; the ancestor of modern police batons.
Kama 鎌
Sickles — farm tools turned close-work blades, usually trained in pairs.
Nunchaku ヌンチャク
Two staves joined by cord — famous, fast, and famously easy to injure yourself with. Learn slowly.
Eku 櫂
The boat oar — proof that Okinawan kobudo can arm you with anything.
Jō 杖
Four-foot staff of jodo — built specifically to defeat the sword by staying out of its arc.
Kusarigama 鎖鎌
Sickle and chain — entangle, then cut; the classical schools' most theatrical weapon.

The “peasant tools” story of kobudo is part history, part romance — some weapons were adapted tools, others were always weapons. Either way, the kata preserve real methods.

Filipino systems

Arnis, kali, and eskrima are names for one family of arts — stick and blade first, empty hand as the natural inheritor of the same angles. The stick is the classroom; the concepts are the lesson.

Baston
Rattan stick — light, strong, and forgiving to train with at speed.
Sinawali
“Weaving” — paired-stick patterns that groove the angles into muscle memory.
Doble baston
Two sticks at once — coordination raised to a new tax bracket.
Espada y daga
Sword and dagger — long and short working together; the same geometry governs stick and empty hand.
Knife work
Realistic, unglamorous, and sobering — much of it is learning what not to be near.
Disarming
The Filipino arts are the world's great disarming systems — practiced with live speed only after years of slow, exact work.

China

Chinese tradition singles out four weapons as the classical curriculum, then adds dozens more — the arsenal of a civilization that never stopped inventing.

Gun 棍
The staff — “the grandfather of all weapons.”
Qiang 槍
The spear — “the king of all weapons,” with its red-tasseled throat.
Dao 刀
The curved saber — “the general of weapons,” broad and slashing.
Jian 劍
The straight double-edged sword — “the gentleman of weapons,” precise and scholarly.
Sanjiegun 三節棍
Three-section staff — folding chaos on a hinge.
Sheng biao 繩鏢
The rope dart — a weighted dart on a cord; tai chi weapon sets give it the slow-motion poetry.

Korea

Jang bong
Long staff — the Korean staff syllabus mirrors the bo's reach games.
Dan bong
Short stick — compact, joint-working, and quick to learn the basics of.
Geum 검
The sword — Haedong Kumdo keeps Korea's own fencing traditions beside imported kendo.
Muyedobotongji heritage
Korea's 1790 military manual records spears, flails, and shield methods — the sourcebook of its modern revivalists.

Europe & the West

Europe wrote its fighting manuals down, and the HEMA revival reads them with steel in mind — armor-proof evidence of how the old masters actually trained.

Longsword
HEMA's flagship: two-handed cutting and thrusting straight out of the 14th-century fight books (Liechtenauer's line and friends).
Sword & buckler
One hand cuts, the little shield punches — the oldest illustrated system in Europe (I.33, ~1300).
Rapier
The Renaissance duelist's thrusting sword, paired with the left hand's dagger or cloak.
Sabre
The cavalry blade — military cutting systems that colonized half the world's dueling codes.
Quarterstaff / poleaxe
Long weapons of the commons and the knights, both; the poleaxe is the armored knight's can-opener.
Canne de combat
France's walking-stick fencing — savate's cousin with a cane.
Fencing (Olympic)
Foil, épée, and sabre — Europe's dueling codes distilled into the fastest sport on two feet.

Choosing your first weapon art

Four honest questions

  • What's nearby? Weapon arts are lineage-sensitive — the best school within reach beats the perfect style three hours away.
  • Sport or classical? Kendo and fencing offer regular, safe competition; kenjutsu and HEMA offer the historical texture. Many schools blend both.
  • What does it cost? Beyond fees: armor (bōgu), practice swords, and padded gear add up — ask before committing.
  • Where will you practice? A staff needs ceiling; a sword needs a clear room. Measure your space before buying anything.

Pair it with something

Weapon arts pair beautifully with an unarmed art — the distances translate in both directions. Judo plus kendo, karate plus kobudo, BJJ plus HEMA longsword: historical enemies, perfect training partners. And when you're ready for forms, the weapon syllabi live in the kobudo kata tables.

Training safety

  • Start with wood, foam, or air. Every weapon tradition has a training ladder — climb it in order. Speed comes years after control.
  • Pad up for contact. Padded sparring weapons, masks, gloves, throat protection — weapons sparring without armor is a bet you eventually lose.
  • Respect the arc. Weapons multiply mistakes: keep distance from everyone behind you, check your space before every swing, and never hand a weapon — set it down for them to pick up.
  • Know the law. Carrying rules for knives, batons, and swords vary enormously by jurisdiction; training tools stay in the training bag.
  • Eyes and fingers first. That's where most training injuries land — which is why masks and control exist.
