Techniques

The vocabulary of fighting, organized by distance: hold your ground (stances and guards), reach out (strikes and kicks), tie up (the clinch), close in (throws and grappling), and survive all of it (defense and falling). These are descriptions, not instructions — learn them on the mat, with a partner who taps early.

Stances the platform everything else stands on

Zenkutsu-dachi 前屈立ち
Front stance — long stance, weight forward, rear leg straight. The karate workhorse for driving forward.
Kokutsu-dachi 後屈立ち
Back stance — weight over the rear leg, built for receiving and deflecting.
Kiba-dachi 騎馬立ち
Horse stance — wide, straddling, thighs loaded. Builds legs and anchors side techniques.
Neko-ashi-dachi 猫足立ち
Cat stance — heel lifted, weight back, ready to kick or spring.
Sanchin-dachi 三戦立ち
Hourglass stance — knees inward, compact and rooted; the Goju-Ryu engine.
Fudo-dachi 不動立ち
“Immovable stance” — between front and horse stance; rooted and ready, the stance of Sochin.
Renoji-dachi レの字立ち
“Letter-L stance” — karate's natural fighting stance, feet tracing an L.
Hanmi 半身
“Half body” — the angled, bladed posture of aikido and kendo; less target, more reach.
Fighting stance
Boxing/kickboxing guard — feet staggered, knees soft, hands high, chin down. Stance of motion rather than rooting.
Jigotai 自護体
Defensive posture in judo — wider and lower than the natural posture, built for attack and defense.
Heisoku-dachi / Musubi-dachi
Attention stances — feet together or heels touching; where bowing and kata begin.
Wrestling stance
Low, staggered, lead hand dangling — built to change level and shoot without being caught upright.

Guards & kamae where you keep your weapons between shots

Chūdan-no-kamae 中段の構え
Kendo's middle guard — point at the opponent's throat. The “water” stance: ready for anything.
Jōdan-no-kamae 上段の構え
High guard — sword raised overhead, all offense, total commitment.
Gedan-no-kamae 下段の構え
Low guard — point lowered; patient, inviting, defensive.
Hassō-no-kamae 八相の構え
Sword held upright beside the head — a ready “oak tree” posture.
Waki-no-kamae 脇の構え
Sword hidden behind the body at the armpit — concealment and surprise.
Orthodox / southpaw
Left foot forward or right foot forward — which hand lives closest to the opponent.
High guard
Gloves welded to the cheeks and forehead; walking forward behind armor.
Philly shell
Lead arm draped across the body, rear glove by the chin — the shoulder-roll defense with a counter built in.

Footwork & movement the fight happens on the feet first

Ayumi-ashi 歩み足
“Walking feet” — natural alternating steps used to close or open distance smoothly.
Tsugi-ashi 継足
Shuffle step — lead foot moves, rear follows; distance changes without crossing the feet.
Suri-ashi 摺足
“Sliding feet” — soles gliding along the floor; the judo and kendo walk that keeps balance intact.
Tai-sabaki 体捌き
Body management — pivoting off the line of attack so the technique meets empty air.
Pivot
Swinging on the lead foot to change angles; the defender's cheapest advantage.
Step-drag
Push with the rear foot, drag the other after — boxing's efficient advance and retreat.
Pendulum / bounce
Rhythmical in-out movement that hides range and loads spring into the legs.
Switch step
Quickly swapping stance to load the “other” kick or power hand.
Mawari-ashi 回り足
Turning movement around a partner — the circle-walking habit of grappling and kendo alike.
Level change
The wrestler's knee drop before every shot — lower hips, straight back, and the takedown begins before the step.

Punches & hand strikes straight lines first

Jab / kizami-zuki 刻み突き
The lead straight — fast, snapping, the rangefinder of boxing.
Cross / gyaku-zuki 逆突き
The reverse straight — rear hand, hip-driven, the power punch.
Hook
Curved punch that comes around the guard; lands on the temple or jaw.
Uppercut
Short rising punch from below, aimed under the chin.
Overhand
A big looping punch thrown over a guarding arm — the brawler's answer to a tight shell.
Superman punch
A feinted kick that becomes an airborne straight — momentum borrowed from the fake.
Spinning backfist
Full turn, arm whipped around on impact; seen across karate, taekwondo, and kickboxing.
Backfist / uraken-uchi 裏拳打ち
Reverse-knuckle snap strike, often off a spin in kata and forms.
Hammer fist / tettsui-uchi 鉄槌打ち
Bottom of the fist, swung like a hammer — padded bones, forgiving accuracy.
Knife hand / shuto-uchi 手刀打ち
Striking with the blade edge of the open hand — the classic “karate chop.”
Ridge hand / haitō-uchi 背刀打ち
Striking with the opposite edge of the open hand, thumb tucked; the mirror of shuto.
Palm heel / teisho 底掌
Push with the base of the palm; safer on the hand, excellent for driving.
Spear hand / nukite 貫手
Fingertip thrust to soft targets — advanced and conditioned.
Headbutt
Short, hard, and risky; legal in Lethwei, banned almost everywhere else, including most self-defense law's good graces. Know it exists; prefer everything else.
Morote-zuki 双手突き
Double punch — both fists fired together, found throughout kata.
Yama-zuki 山突き
“Mountain punch” — a wide U-shaped double punch, the signature of Heian Godan's depths.
Kagi-zuki 钩突き
“Hook punch” — the curved shot of kata, arriving around a guard.
Ura-zuki 裏突き
Close-range short punch, palm up — an uppercut's urban cousin for clinch distance.
Tate-zuki 縦突き
Vertical-fist punch — the Isshin-Ryu standard, wrist aligned to absorb impact.
Shita-zuki 下突き
“Lower punch” — the boxing uppercut's karate name.
Hasami-zuki 挟み突き
“Scissor punch” — both fists closing on the target from either side.
Ippon-ken 一本拳
One-knuckle fist — a conditioned single point for deep targets.
Seiryūtō 青龍刀
“Ox jaw” — striking with the bottom edge of the fist, between hammer and palm.
Kumade 熊手
“Bear hand” — raking claw strike, fingers curled to a point.
Keikō-uchi 鶏冠打ち
“Chicken-beak strike” — a clustered fingertip peck at nerve targets.

Elbows & knees close-range weapons

Horizontal elbow / empi-uchi 肘打ち
Swung across at head height; the hardest, shortest strike in the toolkit.
Upward elbow
Rising elbow under the chin — brutal in the clinch.
Downward elbow
Straight down onto a clinched head; banned in some MMA rulesets for its cutting severity.
Spinning back elbow
Rotation turns defense into an unexpected counter.
Knee strike / hiza-geri 膝蹴り
Driven upward with the hips; the clinch's favorite argument.
Straight knee
Thrust forward through the guard into belly or thigh; the long-range sibling of the rising knee.
Flying knee
Leaping knee from range — fight-ending, high-risk, high-reward.

Kicks the long game

Front kick / mae-geri 前蹴り
Straight snap or thrust with the ball of the foot — the first kick taught, still effective forever.
Roundhouse / mawashi-geri 回し蹴り
Hip-rotated arc, striking with instep (karate) or shin (Muay Thai). The most-used kick in combat sports.
Low kick / calf kick
Roundhouse aimed at thigh or shin; the modern MMA workhorse that quietly removes legs from fights.
Side kick / yoko-geri 横蹴り
Heel-led thrust to the side — power through structure, aimed at ribs or knee.
Oblique / stomp kick
Short, linear stomp to the advancing thigh or shin; a fence, not a knockout blow.
Back kick / ushiro-geri 後ろ蹴り
Straight heel thrust over the shoulder as you turn — a spinning punch made of legs.
Crescent kick / mikazuki-geri 三日月蹴り
Swinging arc with the foot edge; used to strike or to sweep a guard aside.
Axe kick
Leg rises straight, then chops down with the heel.
Hook kick
Side chamber, then hooking heel around the guard — sneaky and precise.
Spinning hook kick
Full rotation into the heel — highlight-reel finisher seen throughout taekwondo and kickboxing.
Question mark kick
A feinted low kick that whips high at the last instant — the guard opens for the wrong attack.
Switch kick
Stance swaps, then the “rear” leg fires instantly — doubling the danger of both legs.
Upkick
From your back, a heel driven upward as an opponent closes — punishment for careless entries.
Teep / push kick
Muay Thai's front thrust — a kick used like a jab to manage distance and break rhythm.
540 kick
Spinning jumping roundhouse — demonstration artistry built on deep fundamentals.
Fumikomi-geri 踏み込み蹴り
Stamping kick — driven down through knee, thigh, or instep; ugly and effective.
Kakato-geri 踵蹴り
Heel kick — short arc or straight thrust with the hardest bone on offer.
Tobi-geri 飛び蹴り
Jumping kicks as a family — nidan-geri, the flying double kick, is the famous one.

Blocks & defense receive, don't just stop

Rising block / age-uke 揚げ受け
Forearm raised against a descending strike to the head.
Inside block / soto-uke 外受け
Forearm sweeps inward across the body, redirecting a punch off-line.
Outside block / uchi-uke 内受け
The mirror move — forearm sweeps outward, opening the attacker's guard.
Downward block / gedan-barai 下段払い
Low sweeping block that also opens kata in most styles.
Knife-hand block / shuto-uke 手刀受け
Open-hand deflection that keeps the other hand free to counter.
Double arm block / juji-uke 十字受け
Crossed forearms block high or low — found across kata everywhere.
Forearm shield
A held, braced arm that lets the punch crash in — absorption instead of redirection.
Slip
Boxing's small head movement — the punch misses by an inch, the counter is already loaded.
Duck
Knees bend, head drops under the hook; come up swinging.
Roll / shoulder roll
Absorb the jab across the shoulder and gloves; energy spent, nothing landed.
Parry
A small redirection with the hand — cheap, fast, and the entry to many traps.
Catch
Glove meets glove — the jab is caught and held long enough to answer it.
Check
Raising the shin to catch kicks — Muay Thai's armor move.
Frame
Forearms pressed against a clinching opponent — a legal fence that buys a breath of space.
Osae-uke 押さえ受け
Pressing block — the arm pins the attack in place rather than deflecting it.
Kake-uke 掛け受け
Hooking block — the wrist catches and drags the attack off-line, opening the counter.
Sukui-uke 掬い受け
Scooping block — the forearm lifts an incoming kick from underneath.
Nagashi-uke 流し受け
Flowing block — a soft glide that redirects rather than meets force.
Morote-uke 双手受け
Augmented block — the second hand reinforces the blocking arm; found throughout kata.
Haishu-uke 背手受け
Back-hand block — deflecting with the back of the open hand.
Bob & weave
Knees bent, head tracing a U under hooks — the pressure fighter's rhythm.
Pull-back
Lean the head an inch out of range — enough to make them miss, enough to punish the miss.

Combinations & feints words into sentences

One-two (jab–cross)
The first sentence of boxing: fast, straight, true. Everything else learns from it.
One-two-three (jab–cross–hook)
Straight lines open the door; the hook walks through it.
Double jab
The second jab lies about the first — rhythm stolen, distance closed.
Body–head
Two to the ribs lift the guard; the third goes over it.
Low–high kick
Shin to the leg makes the hands drop; the head kick takes the invitation.
Check hook
Pivot off the lead foot as an aggressive opponent steps in — they run past their own target.
Pull counter
Lean back an inch off the jab, return over the top with the cross.
Feint
A lie told with the body. The best fighters sell fear one inch at a time.

Clinch the space between punching and throwing

Thai clinch / plum
Both hands behind the head, elbows pinched — control the neck and the knees write the rest.
Collar tie
One hand on the neck, the other controlling the wrist — a steering wheel for the opponent's posture.
Underhook
Your arm threaded under theirs at the armpit — the passport to throws, takedowns, and control.
Overhook
Your arm wrapped over theirs — denying underhooks and feeding wrist control.
Over-under
One underhook each — the classic standing stalemate from which all journeys begin.
Pummeling
Sliding underhooks back and forth in live exchange — the clinch's shadowboxing.
Body lock
Arms locked around the waist — from here, lifts, trips, and slow exhaustion.
Whizzer
Heavy overhook stuffed deep to kill an underhook — the wrestler's doorstop.
Dirty boxing
Short hooks and uppercuts landed from clinch control — legal where the ref allows the tie-up.

Throws & takedowns judo's grammar, wrestling's verbs

O-goshi 大腰
Full hip throw — turn in, load them on the hips, tip.
Uki-goshi 浮腰
“Floating hip” — the softer, diagonal cousin of o-goshi.
Harai-goshi 払腰
“Sweeping hip” — hip contact plus a sweeping leg; judo's big wind.
Hane-goshi 跳腰
“Springing hip” — the hip springs as the leg snaps up behind the knee.
Ippon-seoi-nage 一本背負投
One-arm shoulder throw — the arm goes over your shoulder, the flight follows.
Kata-guruma 肩車
“Shoulder wheel” — the fireman's carry of judo.
O-soto-gari 大外刈
Major outer reap — reap the far leg as you drive the upper body back.
O-uchi-gari 大内刈
Major inner reap — hook the near leg from inside as they step.
Ko-uchi-gari 小内刈
Minor inner reap — a quick heel-hook that punishes lazy feet.
Uchi-mata 内股
Inner-thigh throw — judo's most celebrated big throw.
Tai-otoshi 体落とし
Body drop — a spinning hand technique that trips the body over an extended leg.
Sasae-tsurikomi-ashi 支釣込足
“Propping, drawing ankle” — block the foot, turn the body; they trip over their own step.
Hiza-guruma 膝車
“Knee wheel” — their knee pivots on your foot like a hinge.
Okuri-ashi-harai 送足払
“Sliding foot sweep” — sweep both feet as they slide-step sideways.
De-ashi-barai 出足払
Advancing foot sweep — catch the foot mid-step; timing, not strength.
Tomoe-nage 巴投
“Circle throw” — sit under them and catapult over your head; judo's flying sacrifice.
Sumi-gaeshi 隅返
“Corner reversal” — the tomoe-nage's cousin taken from a one-collar grip.
Tani-otoshi 谷落
“Valley drop” — fall backward beneath them as they push; the counter to aggression.
Double-leg takedown
Wrestling's level change into both legs — the bridge between striking and the ground.
Single-leg takedown
One leg isolated and finished by lift, trip, or drive.
Seoi-nage 背負投
The two-arm shoulder throw — judo's signature, the throw on every poster.
Sode-tsurikomi-goshi 袖釣込腰
"Sleeve-lifting hip" — pull the sleeve, lift, enter, finish on the hip.
Ashi-guruma 足車
"Leg wheel" — the leg blocks, the body pivots over it.
O-guruma 大車
"Large wheel" — the big brother of hiza-guruma, spun over an extended leg.
Ura-nage 裏投
"Back arch" — the suplex of judo; catch the attack and arch them over.
Sumi-otoshi 隅落
"Corner drop" — off-balance them to the corner and step aside.
Yoko-guruma 横車
"Side wheel" — the counter-throw when they attack your hip first.
Uki-waza 浮技
"Floating technique" — a forward sacrifice throw that trades ground for the lead.
Ankle pick
Wrestling's gentlest-looking takedown — a touch of the heel while the head goes the other way.
Duck-under
Slip under the arm to the back — control before they know the round changed.
Snap-down
Yank the head to the mat; the front headlock and all its throws follow.
Front headlock
Chin under your armpit — a control position with chokes, throws, and guillotines inside it.
Suplex
Lift and arch backward — the crowd-pleaser, legal in wrestling and combat sambo.

Pins & holds control before submission

Mount
Seated on the opponent's torso, knees pinning — the top position of maximum control.
Side control
Chest across theirs, perpendicular — the workhorse pin.
Knee-on-belly
Knee posted on the stomach — mobile, heavy, and uncomfortable to carry.
North–south
Chest over their chest, facing their feet — a checkpoint between side controls.
Back control
Behind them with hooks in — where chokes live and fights end.
Half guard
Bottom position with one leg trapped — cramped, tactical, full of sweeps and traps.
Kesa-gatame 袈裟固
“Scarf hold” — arm locked around the neck, leg sprawled for base; judo's first pin.
Kata-gatame 肩固
“Shoulder hold” — arm and head locked together; pressure and a choke in one.
Yoko-shiho-gatame 横四方固
“Side four-quarters” — chest across, hands gripping collar and belt.
Kami-shiho-gatame 上四方固
“Upper four-quarters” — over the head and shoulders, classic pins-competition posture.
Tate-shiho-gatame 縦四方固
“Vertical four-quarters” — judo's name for the mounted pin.

Chokes & joint locks ending the argument gently

Armbar / juji-gatame 十字固め
Hyperextension of the elbow, hips across the shoulder, thumb up.
Kimura
Shoulder rotation lock with a figure-four grip, named for judo's Masahiko Kimura.
Americana
The kimura's mirror — same figure-four, pressure bending the other way. Judo calls it ude-garami.
Omoplata
Shoulder lock applied with the legs — you find it from the guard and finish like a spider.
Triangle choke
Legs form a figure-four around neck and arm — pressure from the thighs, not the arms.
Arm-triangle / kata-gatame choke
Head and one arm squeezed from side control or mount; the judo family calls it kata-gatame-jime.
Rear naked choke / hadaka-jime 裸絞め
Naked choke from the back, forearm across the neck — the finisher of back control.
Collar choke / okuri-eri-jime 送襟絞
“Sliding collar choke” — one hand deep in the lapel, the other feeding from behind.
Ezekiel / sode-guruma-jime 袖車絞
“Sleeve wheel choke” — forearm across the throat, hand gripping your own sleeve; works even inside the guard.
Cross choke / juji-jime 十字絞
Both collars, wrists crossed — the cleanest strangle in the gi.
Guillotine
Front headlock choke — catch a low head, wrap, and raise the arm.
Kneebar
The armbar of the leg — hyperextension of the knee, hips tight to the ankle.
Heel hook
Twisting the knee through the heel — devastating and reserved for experienced grapplers. Tap early; ask often.
Straight ankle lock
Foot in the armpit, pressure across the Achilles — the first legal leg attack in most rulesets.
Toe hold
Figure-four grip on the foot, rotating the knee inward — short lever, big consequence.
Kata-ha-jime 片羽絞
"Single-wing choke" — from back control, one arm wrapped over the shoulder.
Sankaku-jime 三角絞
"Triangle choke" — the same figure-four, called by its judo name on the tatami.
Koshi-jime 腰絞
"Hip choke" — a side-control squeeze that borrows the whole body.
Calf slicer
Turn the shin into a wedge against the calf — rare, legal in some rulesets, memorable always.

Ground movement & solo drills the drills that build the instincts

Shrimping / hip escape
Hips swing away on one side to recover guard or create space — the crawl of grappling.
Bridging
Hips to the ceiling, weight shifted off your back — how pins are shrugged off.
Sprawl
Legs shoot back, hips drop on their shoulders — the answer to every shot.
Technical stand-up
Rising from the ground hand-and-foot first, one eye always on the opponent — the safest get-up there is.
Rolling
Live free grappling at agreed intensity — BJJ's laboratory and judo's randori in one habit.

Falling safely (ukemi) 受け身

The first real skill of any grappling art, and the one that pays off outside the dojo. A breakfall spreads impact over a large surface on the instant it happens: slap the mat with the arm, round the back, exhale, and never post a stiff hand out to catch yourself.

Ushiro-ukemi 後ろ受身
Backward breakfall — the foundation drill of judo and aikido.
Yoko-ukemi 横受身
Side breakfall — how most throws actually put you down.
Mae-ukemi 前受身
Front breakfall — catch the ground along the forearms, turn the head.
Zempo-kaiten 前方回転
Forward roll — flowing over the shoulder and standing back up, judo and aikido's most beautiful drill.

Sensitivity & flow drills learning to feel before seeing

Chi sau 黐手
Wing Chun's “sticky hands” — arms in contact, pressure read through the forearms.
Tui shou 推手
Tai chi's “push hands” — rooted listening: find their balance before they find yours.
Kakie
Goju-Ryu's pushing-hands drill — structure tested under contact.
Randori 乱取り
Judo's “free practice” — live, cooperative-competitive application, the model every live drill borrows.
Flow rolling
Grappling at conversation pace — technique explored without the cost of fighting for it.

Aikido techniques pins and throws of the blending arts

Ikkyō 一教
"First teaching" — arm pinned flat, partner's balance folded to the floor.
Nikyō 二教
"Second teaching" — a wrist fold that politely insists on cooperation.
Sankyō 三教
"Third teaching" — a rising twist along the arm's nerve line.
Yonkyō 四教
"Fourth teaching" — direct pressure on the wrist's tender point.
Gokyō 五教
"Fifth teaching" — the elbow-extension pin, saved for weapons attacks.
Shihōnage 四方投げ
"Four-direction throw" — the arm swept through a full circle, partner carried with it.
Kotegaeshi 小手返し
"Wrist turn-out" — the classic tactical takedown of aikido everywhere.
Iriminage 入身投げ
"Entering throw" — step in behind the attack and the world rotates.
Tenchinage 天地投げ
"Heaven-and-earth throw" — one arm up, one down, balance gone.
Kaitennage 回転投げ
"Rotary throw" — the arm wound like a crank until the trip arrives.

Wing Chun's hand tools the shapes that build the art

Tan sau 攤手
"Dispersing hand" — palm-up deflection on the centerline.
Bong sau 膀手
"Wing arm" — the raised-elbow deflection Wing Chun is famous for.
Pak sau 拍手
"Slapping hand" — a snapping palm push that redirects and opens the guard.
Fok sau 覆手
"Covering hand" — the bridge-holding arm of chi sau.
Huen sau 圈手
"Circling hand" — the wrist rotation that resets every position.
Gaun sau 耕手
"Cultivating hand" — a low chopping deflection, plowing kicks aside.
Biu sau 標指
"Darting hand" — the fingertip thrust of the third form.
Lin wan kuen 連環拳
"Chain punching" — alternate straight punches in an unbroken stream.

Sumo's winning moves kimarite — 82 officially recognized

Yorikiri 寄り切り
"Force out" — belt gripped, opponent walked over the edge. The most common win.
Oshidashi 押し出し
"Push out" — no belt, all palms, out they go.
Uwatenage 上手投げ
"Overarm throw" — the high-grip whirling throw of the giants.
Shitatenage 下手投げ
"Underarm throw" — its low-grip mirror.
Hatakikomi はたき込み
"Slap down" — dodge the charge and slap the shoulders into the clay.
Kotenage 小手投げ
"Armlock throw" — twist the extended arm and let their own charge throw them.
Tsuridashi 吊り出し
"Lift out" — carry the opponent clear of the ring like furniture.

Capoeira's vocabulary the game's signature movements

Ginga
"The sway" — the rhythmic side-step everything else hangs from.
Meia lua de compasso
"Compass half-moon" — the hand-down, heel-whipping kick of capoeira's iconography.
Armada
A spinning inside-to-out heel kick from the ginga.
Queixada
"Jaw" — an outside crescent kick aimed at exactly that.
Rasteira
The low foot-sweep that answers every high kick.
Au
The capoeira cartwheel — transport, evasion, and exclamation mark.
Bananeira
"Banana tree" — the handstand, walked and kicked from.

Weapons overview

A full tour of the weapon arts — Japanese, Okinawan, Filipino, Chinese, and European — lives in its own section: Weapons arts. The short version:

Bo / kon 棒
Six-foot staff — reach, swinging arcs, thrusts; the first and most universal weapon form.
Sai 釵
Truncheon with side prongs — a blocking and trapping tool, not a blade.
Shinai / bokken 竹刀・木剣
Bamboo and wooden practice swords — kendo's and kenjutsu's safe stand-ins for live steel.
Arnis sticks
Rattan sticks of the Filipino arts — paired striking, disarms, and blade logic.
