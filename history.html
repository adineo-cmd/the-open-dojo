History & philosophy

Every training hall is the newest room of a very old building. The word “martial” itself comes from Mars, the Roman god of war — fighting skill has always been wired into the human story. These pages trace where the arts came from, the codes their practitioners lived by, and the ideas that turned fighting into a “way.”

Four regions, four threads

Histories this compressed flatten centuries — but the arcs are real, and each shaped the others.

China

Legend puts fighting systems at the Yellow Emperor's court; history gives us wrestling and archery by the Zhou dynasty. The Shaolin temple's monks were famous fighters by the Tang era. The internal arts — tai chi, bagua, xingyi — crystallized in the 17th–19th centuries, and the 20th brought both suppression and reinvention: the modern wushu performance sport, and the diaspora that carried kung fu worldwide.

Okinawa & Japan

Okinawa's karate grew from local fighting methods and Chinese influence; when weapons were restricted on the islands, the arts trained quietly, family by family. In 1922 Gichin Funakoshi demonstrated karate in Tokyo and it joined Japan's rising culture of modern budō — judo had been founded by Jigoro Kano in 1882, aikido followed in the 20th century, and after WWII American GIs carried all of them home.

Korea

Old Korea wrestled (ssireum), kicked (taekkyeon), and recorded hand methods in its military manuals. The Japanese occupation suppressed native practice; after liberation in 1945, the new kwans — drawing on taekkyon memory and karate lineage — merged into what was named taekwondo in 1955. It became an Olympic sport in 2000 and Korea's calling card.

The West & Brazil

Boxing codified into the prize ring and the Queensberry rules (1867); savate grew in French ports; catch wrestling thrived in Lancashire. Judo's arrival seeded BJJ in Brazil, whose Gracie family built a ground-fighting empire — and when their challenge matches went on cable in 1993 as the Ultimate Fighting Championship, the modern mixed martial arts era began.

Warrior codes

Wherever skill with violence existed, someone wrote rules for holding it. The codes differ in culture and agree in spirit.

Bushidō 武士道

The way of the warrior: rectitude, courage, benevolence, respect, honesty, honor, loyalty. Formalized late, lived early — the ethic of Japan's samurai and the soil later budō grew in.

The Hwarang code

Korea's Silla youth corps trained by five commandments — loyalty to king and country, respect for parents, fidelity to friends, no retreat in battle, no unjust killing. Taekwondo's student oath still echoes it.

Chivalry & the West

Europe's knightly ideals — protect the weak, keep the faith, mastery as service — rhyme with the Eastern codes. The tournament ring and the dojo are cousins.

Dō vs. jutsu: technique and way

One syllable separates two philosophies. Jutsu (術) means method — jujutsu, kenjutsu: arts organized around making the technique work, usually for war. Dō (道) means way — judo, kendo, aikido, karate-dō: the same movements reframed as a path for building the person, with the fighting as curriculum and character as the goal.

The turn happened when Japan modernized and battlefield arts needed a new purpose — Kano's judo showed in 1882 that a school could be simultaneously modern, safe, and rigorous, and every art since has negotiated where it sits on the jutsu-to-dō spectrum. Your own gym probably teaches both at once: the throw that works, and the person you become learning it.

Shin-gi-tai (心技体): mind, technique, body — the Japanese training triangle. A complete martial artist works all three vertices; neglect one and the other two eventually sag. Compare China's wude (martial virtue) and Korea's dojang spirit: three countries, one conclusion.

Mind & meditation

Fighting is one of the few activities where losing your thoughts costs you instantly — which is why every tradition found a way to train the mind deliberately.

Mokuso 黙想

The kneeling silence that opens and closes Japanese classes — a minute to set the day down before picking up the sword (or the bag).

Mushin 無心

“No mind” — action without hesitation. Not emptiness: thousands of repetitions settling into a mind quiet enough to let the hands speak first.

Zanshin 残心

“Remaining mind” — the awareness that survives the technique. In archery and swordsmanship it is graded as seriously as the shot itself.

The ethics of skill

Funakoshi's dojo kun — the five precepts recited in karate schools worldwide — ask students to seek perfection of character, be faithful, endeavor, respect others, and refrain from violent behavior. Notice the shape of that list: one fighting item, four human ones. The old assumption runs throughout: technique amplifies whatever is already in you.

Modern practice inherits the promise: instructors earn authority by what they refuse; senior students set the temperature for contact; and every art's first lesson is the same — the strongest person in the room should be the calmest. When schools forget this, the belt system becomes commerce and the code becomes a wall plaque. The belt page has the practical version of this warning.

Women in the martial arts

The dojo floor has always been co-ed in spirit, even when the certificates weren't. A few names from a long history:

Keiko Fukuda

Studied directly under Jigoro Kano, defied expectations to remain a professional judoka all her life, and in 2011 became the first woman awarded 10th dan in judo — at 98 years old.

Rusty Kanokogi

An American judoka who, barred from a 1959 YMCA tournament after winning as one, spent decades fighting for women's competition — and bankrolled the first Women's World Judo Championships herself.

The Olympic record

Women's judo arrived at the 1992 Barcelona Games; taekwondo included women from its 2000 debut; women's boxing followed in 2012 — and today women's divisions anchor every major federation.

Deeper history holds more: Okinawan tradition credits women with founding roles in some family arts, Wing Chun's origin legend is a woman, and archery traditions worldwide trained both sexes from the start.

The martial arts on screen

Few arts grew without their cinema. Akira Kurosawa's Seven Samurai (1954) taught the world what swordsmanship looks like on film; Enter the Dragon (1973) made Bruce Lee a global figure months after his death and sent a generation racing to kung fu schools; The Karate Kid (1984) did the same for suburban karate dojos across the West.

The influences ran both ways: Jackie Chan's inventiveness, Donnie Yen's Ip Man films (2008–), Tony Jaa's Ong-Bak (2003), and The Matrix (1999) — choreographed by Yuen Woo-ping — reshaped what audiences expect from action, and what newcomers expect from their first class. Enjoy the movies; the library will still be here to correct them.

The modern era

A quick chronology of how the arts became global:

YearMilestone
1882Jigoro Kano founds judo — the template for modern, codified, globally exportable martial arts.
1922Funakoshi demonstrates karate in Tokyo; Okinawan art meets mainland Japan.
1955The name “taekwondo” is adopted for Korea's unified art.
1964Judo debuts at the Tokyo Olympics — the first martial art in the Games.
1970s–80sKarate and kung fu movies carry the arts into living rooms worldwide; dojo numbers explode.
1993The first UFC puts styles against styles and asks the oldest question: what actually works?
2000Taekwondo becomes a full Olympic sport in Sydney.
2021Karate debuts at the Tokyo Olympics; modern HEMA and BJJ enjoy historic growth.

Where it's heading: the old walls between arts keep thinning. Grapplers cross-train striking; traditional schools borrow judo's live-training honesty; HEMA reads 600-year-old books with sports-science tools. The library will keep updating as the story does — tell us what we missed.
