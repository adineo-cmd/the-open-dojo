Library / Styles

Martial arts styles

Every art in this catalog answers the same question differently: what do you do when someone wants to fight you? Below, the featured profiles give you a feel for each answer, and the master list goes broad — nearly 140 arts and traditions across seven regions, by method, and by what to expect in a class.

Featured styles

Ten arts that anchor most of the world's training halls. Each card shows the style's own writing — every tradition names itself first.

空手道
Okinawa, refined in mainland Japan

Karate

“Empty hand” — an Okinawan blend of indigenous fighting methods and Chinese influence that mainland Japan organized into modern schools (ryū). Training rests on three legs: kihon (basics), kata (forms), and kumite (sparring). Expect deep stances, direct linear strikes, and a culture of exact repetition.

Striking Kata Major styles: Shotokan · Goju-Ryu · Shito-Ryu · Wado-Ryu · Kyokushin
跆拳道
Korea · 1940s–50s

Taekwondo

Korea's most exported art, formally consolidated after independence and shaped by karate lineage plus older Korean kicking traditions. The name means “the way of the foot and fist,” and the feet do most of the talking: fast linear kicks, spinning kicks, and jumping techniques. Two main governing families — Kukkiwon/WT (Olympic) and ITF — differ in forms and sparring rules.

Striking Kicks Olympic sport Poomsae / patterns
柔道
Japan · founded 1882

Judo

Jigoro Kano distilled older jujutsu schools into a method he called “the gentle way” — winning by yielding, using balance and timing rather than force. Throws and sweeps score highest; ground work covers pins, armlocks, and chokes. Judo became the first martial art in the Olympics and the root system for much of modern grappling.

Grappling Throws Pins & submissions Olympic sport
Jiu-Jitsu Brasileiro
Brazil · early 1900s

Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu

Built on judo ground work that the Gracie family adapted and refined, BJJ holds that a smaller person can control and submit a larger one through position: guard, mount, back control. Chokes and joint locks end fights on the mat. Its belt ladder — white through black, with stripes — has become the cultural template for grappling gyms everywhere.

Grappling Ground fighting Submissions
มวยไทย
Thailand

Muay Thai

Thailand's national sport grew out of centuries of battlefield arts into ring fighting called “the art of eight limbs”: two fists, two elbows, two knees, two shins. Its clinch work — controlling the head and neck while striking with knees — is unmatched, and its roundhouse kick delivered with the shin is a benchmark of power.

Striking Clinch Eight limbs
詠春
China · Guangdong (Canton)

Wing Chun

A compact Southern Chinese system built for close range: the centerline theory, straight punches, low kicks, and sticky-hands (chi sau) sensitivity that trains reaction by touch. Familiar worldwide through Ip Man and his most famous student, Bruce Lee, who later folded its ideas into Jeet Kune Do.

Striking Close range Chi sau
合気道
Japan · early 1900s

Aikido

Morihei Ueshiba's art aims to resolve conflict without destroying the attacker: joint locks, pinning throws, and blending movement that redirects an attack instead of blocking it. Weapons forms (staff and wooden sword) shape much of its movement. Grading emphasizes flowing technique and ukemi — receiving technique safely.

Joint locks Throws Non-competitive
קרב מגע
Israel · 1940s onward

Krav Maga

“Contact combat,” developed by Imi Lichtenfeld and taught first to Israel's armed forces, then to civilians. It borrows whatever works — boxing punches, judo throws, BJJ ground defense — and strips it to what survives stress: aggressive simultaneous defense and attack, targeting, and escaping. Civilian training centers on self-defense scenarios rather than sport.

Self-defense Scenario training No sport rules
唐手道
Korea

Tang Soo Do

“The way of the China hand” — a Korean art with strong Shotokan ancestry that predates and parallels modern taekwondo. It keeps traditional forms, rigid discipline, and a balance of hand and foot techniques, and it was never fully absorbed into the Olympic sport family. Chuck Norris made it famous in the West.

Striking Traditional forms
Capoeira
Brazil · by enslaved Africans

Capoeira

An Afro-Brazilian art disguised as dance — or dance disguised as fighting; history made it both. Practitioners play in a roda (circle) to berimbau music, weaving kicks, sweeps, acrobatics, and evasion into a conversation with a partner. Training builds balance, rhythm, and mobility few other arts match.

Kicks & acrobatics Music & rhythm

Master list, by region

The wider catalog — over a hundred arts and traditions. Placement reflects where an art took shape; many arts since spread far past their homelands. Skip ahead: Japan & Okinawa · Korea · China · Around the world · Ancient & folk · South & SE Asia · Modern hybrids.

How complete is this? Honestly: no catalog can hold every martial art. Thousands exist — every village's wrestling, every family's kung fu lineage, every army's close-combat manual. This library documents nearly 140 named arts plus the techniques, syllabi, and terminology you'll actually meet in training halls. If your art is missing, that's an invitation: write it in and help the map grow.

Styles by method

A different axis: what the art actually trains. Most people choose by method first, style second.

Striking arts

Karate, taekwondo, boxing, kickboxing, Muay Thai, sanda, savate. Distance management, punch and kick mechanics, footwork, and defense with hands, feet, knees, and elbows.

Grappling arts

Judo, BJJ, wrestling, sambo, luta livre, shuai jiao. Takedowns and throws, clinch control, pins, and submissions — fighting where the distance collapses.

Weapons arts

Kendo, iaido, kenjutsu, arnis/kali, krabi-krabong, bojutsu. Sword, staff, stick, and blade traditions that sharpen distance, timing, and intent.

Hybrid & tactical

MMA, krav maga, jeet kune do, sambo, HEMA sparring. Systems that mix ranges on purpose, often with pressure testing as the standard of truth.

Internal & flowing

Tai chi, baguazhang, xingyiquan, aikido, taekkyeon. Emphasis on structure, relaxation, and sensitivity; many practitioners train them alongside harder arts.

Choosing your first school

The art matters less than the instruction. A great teacher of an average style beats the reverse in every case that matters.

Checklist for your first dojo visit

  • Watch a class before joining. Reputable schools expect it. Look for how instructors correct individuals, not just the group.
  • Ask about lineage and rules. Who do the instructors rank under? Is sparring (or rolling) supervised, progressive, and consent-based?
  • Check the beginner path. Is there a structured way to learn the basics, or are new students thrown to the senior class on day one?
  • Count the cost, all of it. Monthly fees, grading fees, uniforms, gear. Surprise fees are a bad sign; clear ones are a good one.
  • Try it twice. The first class is nerves; the second tells you what the training actually feels like.

For parents

Martial arts are one of the best activities a kid can stick with — structure, respect, and a scoreboard nobody has to win. Look for age-appropriate classes (many schools start around 5–7), a calm instructor-to-student ratio, games that hide drills, and a culture where contact is introduced gradually with proper padding. A school that welcomes parents to watch a full class is a school that's proud of what happens in it.
