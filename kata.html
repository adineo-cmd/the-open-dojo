Kata & forms

A kata is a choreographed fight against imaginary opponents — the textbook a striking art writes about itself. Karate calls them kata (型), taekwondo calls them poomsae or teul, tang soo do calls them hyung, judo keeps its own set of formal demonstrations; the idea is shared. Use these tables to review names, meanings, and the order you'll usually meet them in.

Shotokan Karate

The most widely practiced karate syllabus. The five Heian kata carry a student from yellow belt to the doorstep of brown; from there the syllabus widens quickly. Belt placements below reflect a common dojo order — every organization sequences slightly differently.

KataKanjiMeaningTypically learned
Taikyoku Shodan太極初段“First cause” — a beginner's pattern of down-blocks and stepping punches.White belt (many dojos)
Heian Shodan平安初段“Peaceful mind, first level.” The foundation kata: blocks, lunge punches, turns.Yellow / orange
Heian Nidan平安二段Introduces side kicks, double blocks, and first open-hand techniques.Orange
Heian Sandan平安三段Short and staccato; features kiba-dachi and spinning movements.Orange / green
Heian Yondan平安四段Slow, deliberate openings with knee strikes and double hammer fists.Green
Heian Godan平安五段The series finale: a jump, press-block sequence, and quick timing changes.Green
Tekki Shodan鉄騎初段“Iron horse.” Fought entirely in the straddling kiba-dachi; sidestepping close-range defense.Blue / purple
Bassai Dai披塞大“To storm a fortress.” Converts blocked attacks into overwhelming counterattack.Brown
Kanku Dai観空大“Viewing the sky,” from its opening. The longest of the classic Sentei kata.Brown / 1st kyu
Empi燕飛“Flight of the swallow” — dropping, rising movement like a bird over water.Brown
Jion慈恩Named for a temple; a straightforward, powerful kata prized in tournaments.Brown
Hangetsu半月“Half moon,” for its stance and breathing method; slow tension, fast finish.Brown
Jitte十手“Ten hands” — mastery suggests the power of ten fighters.Brown / black
Gankaku岩鶴“Crane on a rock,” balancing on one leg throughout.Black belt
Tekki Nidan / Sandan鉄騎二段・三段Second and third rides of the iron-horse series.Black belt
Bassai Sho披塞小The “lesser” Bassai, built on deflecting with the open hand.Black belt
Kanku Sho観空小A shorter, jump-heavy companion to Kanku Dai.Black belt
Chinte珍手“Rare hands” — unusual strikes preserved from older practice.Black belt
Unsu雲手“Cloud hands” — sweeping circular motion ending in a famous jump.Black belt
Sochin壯鎭“Strength and calm,” anchored in fudo-dachi (immovable stance).Black belt
Nijushiho二十四步“Twenty-four steps,” with wave-like rhythm throughout.Black belt
Gojushiho Dai / Sho五十四歩大・小“Fifty-four steps” — two advanced variations of the same theme.Senior black belt
Meikyo明鏡“Bright mirror” — polished, precise, with a unique hoisting jump.Senior black belt
Wankan王冠“King's crown” — the shortest advanced kata, one breath of technique.Senior black belt
Ji'in慈陰“Temple ground” — another temple-named kata in the old line.Black belt

Counts and order vary by organization (JKA, JKS, SKIF, and others sequence the black-belt katas differently).

Taekwondo forms

Two main families: the Kukkiwon/WT poomsae — the eight Taegeuk forms followed by nine black-belt forms — and the ITF's 24 patterns (teul), one for each hour of the day a fighter might be called upon. Both name their forms after deep symbolism.

Taegeuk Pal Jang☷ Gon (地)Earth — the foundation and root of all Taegeuk; the last before black belt testing.Red belt

Goju-Ryu Karate

“The hard and soft way.” Goju's twelve core kata alternate between tense, breathing forms and flowing ones, and the syllabus is counted in Chinese-influenced numbers.

KataKanjiMeaning
Gekisai Dai Ichi / Ni撃砕“To attack and crush” — beginner kata written in 1940 to standardize teaching.
Sanchin三戦“Three battles” — breath, body, and mind unified in tension.
Tensho転掌“Rotating palms” — the soft partner to Sanchin's hardness.
Saifa砕破“Smash and tear” — close-range grabbing and striking.
Seiyunchin制引戦“To grip, pull, and fight” — no kicks, all control.
Shisochin四向戦“Fighting in four directions” with open-hand blocks.
Sanseiru三十六“Thirty-six,” a number-bearing kata of power techniques.
Seipai十八“Eighteen” — a tournament favorite with seizing throws.
Kururunfa久留頓破“Holding on long, striking suddenly” — evasive softness.
Suparinpei壱百零八“One hundred and eight” — the highest, longest kata of the line.

Shito-Ryu Karate

Kenwa Mabuni's school is the encyclopedist of karate: it deliberately preserved both the Naha-te (Goju-like) and Shuri-te (Shotokan-like) lines, and keeps the longest syllabus of the major styles — sixty or more kata. A representative selection:

Pinan Shodan – Godan平安The five “peaceful mind” forms, the backbone of early grading.
Bassai Dai / Sho披塞大・小“Storming the fortress,” shared with Shotokan's line.
Kosokun Dai / Sho公相君What Shotokan calls Kanku — “viewing the sky.”
Seienchin制引戦Long, grinding kata of pulls, grips, and stances.
Wanshu汪楫Named for a Chinese envoy; Shotokan's Empi comes from this line.
Nipaipo二十八歩“Twenty-eight steps” — a fast, linear Shito-Ryu signature.
Rohai鷺牌“Sign of the heron” — one-legged poise and sudden turns.
Jion / Ji'in / Jitte慈恩・慈陰・十手The temple-named trio of Tomari-te descent.
Niseishi二十四“Twenty-four steps”; Shotokan renamed it Nijushiho.
Unshu雲手“Cloud hands,” cousin of Shotokan's Unsu.
Sanchin / Tensho三戦・転掌The breathing pair, kept from the Naha-te line.
Saifa / Seipai / Kururunfa / Suparinpei砕破・十八・久留頓破・壱百零八The advanced Naha-te set, shared with Goju-Ryu.
Aoyagi / Juroku青柳・十六Modern compositions written by Mabuni for teaching (“green willow,” “sixteen”).

More karate lineages

Five more syllabi students most often ask about. Names differ from Shotokan because all karate lineages grew from the same Okinawan root forms.

Wado-Ryu

“The way of harmony.” Jujutsu-influenced body shifting plus a classic Okinawan set:

  • Pinan Shodan – Godan (平安, “peaceful mind”)
  • Kushanku (公相君)
  • Naihanchi (内歩進) — three versions
  • Seishan (十三, “thirteen”)
  • Chinto (鎮東)
  • Wanshu (汪楫)
  • Rohai (鷺牌, “heron on a rock”)
  • Niseishi (二十四, “twenty-four”)
  • Bassai (披塞) and Jion (慈恩)

Shorin-Ryu

The light, fast Okinawan parent line. Core set:

  • Kihon kata (basic form)
  • Pinan I – V
  • Naihanchi Shodan – Sandan
  • Passai (披塞, “to storm a fortress”)
  • Kusanku (公相君)
  • Chinto (鎮東)
  • Gojushiho (五十四歩, “fifty-four steps”)

Isshin-Ryu

Okinawan style of the vertical fist. Eight empty-hand kata:

  • Seisan (十三)
  • Seiunchin (制引戦)
  • Naihanchi (内歩進)
  • Wansu (汪楫)
  • Chinto (鎮東)
  • Kusanku (公相君)
  • Sunsu — the style's own signature kata
  • Sanchin (三戦)

Kyokushin

Full-contact karate with a graded ladder from simple to severe:

  • Taikyoku Sono Ichi – San (太極)
  • Pinan Sono Ichi – Go (平安)
  • Gekisai Dai / Sho (撃砕)
  • Yantsu and Tsuki no Kata
  • Sanchin (三戦) and Tensho (転掌)
  • Saiha / Saifa (砕破)
  • Kanku (観空) and Seipai (十八)
  • …into the senior black-belt forms

Uechi-Ryu

A Naha-te lineage built on three animals — tiger, crane, dragon — and the breathing kata Sanchin. Eight empty-hand kata, many named for numbers:

  • Sanchin (三戦, “three battles”) — the heart of the style, practiced at every grade
  • Kanshiwa and Kanshu — beginner-to-intermediate forms
  • Seichin
  • Seisan (十三, “thirteen”) — the oldest form of the line
  • Sanseiru (三十六, “thirty-six”)
  • Seiryu (十六, “sixteen”)
  • Kanchin

Kata beyond karate

Kata are not only a striking-art habit. Judo keeps a small museum of codified kata — formal demonstrations of principle rather than belt requirements for most students — and the internal Chinese arts practice their whole curriculum as solo taolu.

Judo kataKanjiWhat it preserves
Nage no kata投の形The “forms of throwing” — fifteen throws in five sets, the classic grading demonstration.
Katame no kata固の形The “forms of grappling” — fifteen pins, chokes, joint locks, and escapes.
Kime no kata極の形“Forms of decision” — self-defense scenarios from kneeling and standing, the old jujutsu memory.
Ju no kata柔の形“Gentle forms” — flowing lessons on yielding, often taught to younger students.
Koshiki no kata古式の形“Antique forms” — armored-combat movement preserved from the Kito-ryu.
Itsutsu no kata五の形“Five forms” of pure principle — famously left unfinished by Kano himself.

Aikido & the weapon forms

Aikido teaches its principles through paired weapons kata — wooden sword (bokken), staff (jo), and knife-taking — which shape the empty-hand movement: every strike is a sword cut wearing a glove.

Tai chi & the taolu

In the Chinese internal arts the solo form is the syllabus: the long Yang-style form of 108 movements, Chen's cannon-fist (paochui), and weapon sets of straight sword (jian) and saber (dao). See the weapons section.

How to practice a kata

The form is the map; practice is the walking. A loop that works:

  1. Learn the shape. Follow the sequence exactly — directions, stances, hand positions — before adding anything else.
  2. Slow it down. Perform at half speed and feel every transition. Errors hide at full speed and surface at half.
  3. Name the moves. Say the technique names to yourself as you go; it welds the kata to the technique library.
  4. Add intent. Every block meets an attack; every strike meets a target. Imagine the opponent or the kata stays a dance.
  5. Then test it. Full power, full speed, one breath — and let your instructor tear it apart. That's the point.

Forms and reality: kata store technique and train structure — they are not sparring. Their lessons land only when paired with controlled partner work, which is why every serious syllabus trains kihon, kata, and kumite together.
